# I. Supply-side / counterpart pain (hidden stakeholders)

Researcher: desk research agent. Run date: 2026-10-22. About 24 web calls.
Labels: **FACT** = I opened the page and the claim is in it. **HYPOTHESIS** = the claim comes from a search snippet or summary only, or is my own inference.
Caveat: WebSearch results are summarised by a model. Every "snippet" row below needs to be opened before anyone cites it.
Pantip, Facebook groups and LINE OpenChat were not reachable. The operator's voice is therefore under-represented: most sources are news coverage written from the victim's side.

### Mechanism insight (HYPOTHESIS)
- **Follower count works against small honest operators.** Scammers buy ads and followers, so a fake page can look more legitimate than the real one (11,000 vs 425 followers). The heuristic that authorities and media keep recommending ("check followers") therefore penalises the smallest honest players.
- **The official advice ("book via OTA, avoid Facebook") moves bookings to OTAs**, which typically charge about 15–20% commission (my assumption; not sourced here). Honest direct-booking operators lose either way: through impersonation, or through the commission when travellers follow the safety advice.
- **Takedown is whack-a-mole.** Reporting to Facebook does not stop new clones. The operator's only lever is repeated warning posts, which cost unpaid time.
- **The legitimacy signals available to a homestay are weak or costly:** a company bank account (requires incorporating), a verified badge (monthly fee, or document hurdles), a DoT licence (applies only to tour operators, not homestays), and video calls (owner time). A personal-name bank account, which is normal for a micro homestay, is exactly what the anti-scam guidance flags as a mule-account signal.
- Victims usually discover the fraud on arrival or close to the check-in date. Their anger may first land on the real property (inference; no source quotes operators on this).

### WTP signal (counterpart side)
- Indirect only: Meta Verified has been sold in Thailand since 2023 at about 280–330 baht/month (HYPOTHESIS, snippet). I found no data on how many Thai SMEs or homestays subscribe.
- Operators bear costs in kind: warning posts, police complaints (Vranda), and offering video calls. **No direct WTP evidence found.**

### Unknowns
- How many bookings or how much revenue real operators lose to fakes, and whether travellers now refuse to transfer money to them. No operator-side survey was found.
- Current Thai pricing for Meta Verified for business pages, and the LINE OA verification criteria and cost in Thailand.
- Whether homestays and pool villas hold any registration that travellers can check (the hotel licence under the Hotel Act, or homestay standard certification).

### Mechanism insight (HYPOTHESIS)
- Enforcing a no-show penalty is legally possible only if the terms were stated **before** booking. Most Thai SMEs book through Facebook or LINE chat with no written terms, so in practice they have no recourse. A deposit taken by bank transfer is their only enforcement tool.
- This is the **mirror image of S1.** Operators need a prepaid deposit to protect against no-shows, but travellers increasingly refuse to prepay strangers because of scam fear. Both sides want a trusted escrow or record of the terms, but neither can supply it to the other.
- Lawyers say suing is "not worth it", so disputes are settled through social-media shaming or by absorbing the loss.

### WTP signal (counterpart side)
- Upscale restaurants already pay for TableCheck-type systems with deposit capture (HYPOTHESIS, inferred from the listing). There are no data for small or SME restaurants. **None found for vans, tours or homestays.**

### Unknowns
- Thai no-show rates for any segment; dispute volumes for van and tour deposits; how common written cancellation terms are in LINE/FB bookings.

### Mechanism insight (HYPOTHESIS)
- **Regulated fare below cost for short or congested trips, plus the daily car rent or instalment, plus fuel** makes refusing passengers or negotiating a flat fare the rational choice per trip. Tourists (who tip and pay flat fares) are the most profitable customers, which explains the pattern of refusing Thai passengers on the meter while touting to foreigners.
- **The policy gives the industry a collective-action trap.** Officials tie fare increases to fewer refusal complaints, but each driver's individual incentive is to keep refusing. Honest drivers carry the reputational cost of the dishonest ones with no way to signal they are different, apart from moving to apps.
- Ride-hailing apps and black-plate cars squeeze metered income further, which pushes the remaining street-hail drivers toward tourists and fixed prices.

### WTP signal (counterpart side)
- Drivers collectively lobby for higher fares and cheaper gas rather than paying for reputation tools. Many already pay app commissions (Grab/Bolt) to get passengers; the size of those commissions was not sourced here. **No direct WTP found for a reputation or verification signal.**

### Unknowns
- Post-2023 driver income and cost data; tuk-tuk economics (no sources found); whether any honest-driver collectives or LINE groups exist; the status of the 2025–26 fare-rise proposal.

### Findings
| Claim | Source URL + date | Opened? | Status | Confidence |

| Landlord case: a tenant on a 6-month lease left at night without notice and without paying rent, water or electricity. The landlord holds the deposit and asks what to do. | https://legardy.com/free-qna/29640 (Q about 2025; answers 15 Oct 2025 and 1 Jun 2026) | Yes | FACT | High (anecdote) |

| News reports of tenants leaving units wrecked; in one Phuket case a foreign tenant left the unit damaged and the deposit (about 32,000 baht) was disputed. | https://www.thairath.co.th/news/society/2856545 ; https://www.dailynews.co.th/news/5930128/ | No (snippets) | HYPOTHESIS | Medium |

### Mechanism insight (HYPOTHESIS)
- Since the 2018 OCPB rules, landlords with 5 or more units can no longer over-collect deposits. Their buffer is capped at about 1 month's deposit and must be refunded fast, so **the burden of proof sits with the landlord**. Without move-in photos, an itemised list and chat records, deductions can be challenged as unfair.
- Recovering anything beyond the deposit requires a civil suit, which is uneconomic for a few thousand baht. A tenant leaving at night is therefore a sunk loss, and landlords respond by screening tenants or keeping the deposit aggressively. Aggressive retention is what tenants experience as "the landlord stole my deposit".
- The records landlords keep: the contract (with the tenant's address), chat logs, meter readings, and photos. Lawyers tell them to keep these, which implies many do not do so systematically.

### WTP signal (counterpart side)
- Dorm-management software exists in Thailand (not researched here). **None found from sources opened in this run.**

### Unknowns
- How common night move-outs and damage are; whether landlords take systematic move-in or move-out photos; the adoption and price of Thai dorm-management tools.

## Cross-cutting notes
- **S1 and S2 are two sides of one trust deadlock**: operators need prepayment to deter no-shows, while travellers fear prepaying because of impersonation. The official advice ("use OTAs") resolves it by routing money through intermediaries that take a commission (HYPOTHESIS).
- Strongest opened evidence: S1 (three opened Thai PBS and InfoQuest cases with numbers) and S3 (opened Thai PBS 2023 article, fare table). Weakest: S5 (no Thai source) and S2 for vans and tours.
